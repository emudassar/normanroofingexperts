## Rural roofing inside the city limits

The Franklin–Denver area is where Norman stops being a city and starts being country,
deliberately so. The city's long-range land use planning treats this belt as clustered rural
residential, broadly one dwelling per ten acres with a two-acre minimum lot, with the twin
aims of protecting the Little River watershed and its tributaries and maintaining physical
separation between Norman and Moore.

For a homeowner that means space, quiet and a longer drive to anywhere. For a roofer it means
a different job entirely: no two properties alike, multiple buildings per parcel, mixed
materials, full exposure and access as a real constraint rather than an afterthought.

## The two-material property

Almost every parcel out here has both roofing systems on it, and they need to be understood
separately.

**The house** is usually asphalt shingle on a conventional structure. It fails the way any
Oklahoma roof fails: sun on the south and west slopes, hail, and wind breaking the seal
strips. What's different is exposure: with no windbreak, the wind reaching a house here
arrives with more energy than the same wind reaching a house in a built-up neighbourhood.

**The outbuildings**, shop, barn, equipment shed and detached garage, are almost always
exposed-fastener metal, and they fail at the fasteners. Each screw is a hole through the
panel sealed by a neoprene washer under the screw head. UV degrades the washer. Thermal
cycling backs the screw out fractionally, year after year. Eventually every screw is a slow
leak, and there are thousands of them on a large shop.

The good news is that this is repairable. A full refasten, meaning larger-diameter screws driven
into fresh purchase, new closures at ridge and eaves, sealant renewed at laps and
penetrations, restores a sound building at a small fraction of re-sheeting cost. We'd rather
do that and tell you the panels have fifteen years in them than sell you a new roof you
didn't need.

## Access: get it agreed before the truck is loaded

This is not a trivial point on rural property. The things that actually derail jobs out here:

- A drive too long or too narrow for a delivery truck with a boom
- Gates or cattle guards with weight limits
- Ground that carried a truck in July and won't in April
- Overhead lines on the approach
- Septic tanks and drain fields where a container would naturally sit
- Livestock, and the need to keep gates shut and debris off pasture

None of it is difficult. All of it costs a day if it's discovered on the morning of the job.
We walk it at the survey.

## Documentation matters more when nobody sees the roof

An in-town roof gets looked at by neighbours, delivery drivers and anyone walking past. A
barn roof on forty acres gets looked at when something inside gets wet. By then a
fastener problem has become a rust problem, and rust is a replacement rather than a repair.

An annual walk of every roof on the property, and a proper look after any significant storm,
is the highest-return maintenance available on a rural parcel. Our
