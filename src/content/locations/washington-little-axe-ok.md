## Two communities, one set of rural roofing realities

Little Axe and Washington sit in different directions from Norman and in different counties.
Little Axe is the unincorporated community to the east, in Cleveland County, with Lake
Thunderbird and its surrounding wooded land nearby and its own school district. Washington is
a small town to the south in McClain County, with farm and acreage property around it.

What they share is the position of being just outside: outside city limits, outside the
routes most Norman contractors drive, and outside the areas that get inspected first after a
storm. They also share the rural property pattern: individually built houses of widely
varying age, and metal outbuildings on nearly every parcel with land attached.

## What's different about the wooded east side

Most rural roofing around Norman is about exposure: open ground, unbroken wind, full sun.
The land east toward Lake Thunderbird is the opposite case, and it produces a different
problem set.

**Abrasion.** A branch resting on or lightly touching a roof scrubs it every time the wind
blows. Over a season that strips granules along the contact line and creates a bald streak
that ages several times faster than the rest of the slope.

**Persistent damp.** Slopes that get little direct sun stay wet longer after every rain.
That's where algae streaking establishes, cosmetic mostly, and where moss establishes,
which is not cosmetic. Moss holds water against the shingle surface indefinitely and works
its way under the edges.

**Valley loading.** Leaf litter washes into valleys and packs. A packed valley holds a strip
of permanently damp debris against the shingles where water volume is already highest. It's
one of the most reliably damaging and most easily prevented problems we see.

**Ice storm risk.** Central Oklahoma gets significant ice events, and the combination of
heavy tree cover and ice loading is how roofs get holes in them. An overhanging limb is a
maintenance issue for fifty weeks a year and a structural one for the other two.

The remedy for all four is the same and it isn't roofing: trim back anything overhanging the
roof, and keep valleys and gutters clear through autumn.

## Manufactured housing, done correctly

Both communities have a meaningful amount of manufactured and modular housing, and it's worth
being direct about it because it's frequently done badly.

The critical variable is pitch. Asphalt shingles rely on slope to shed water; below the
manufacturer's minimum slope they will let water in under wind-driven rain no matter how well
they're laid. A great many manufactured homes have roofs at or below that threshold, and
shingling them anyway is common because it's what the crew on site knows how to do.

The correct answers at low slope are a membrane system or metal roofing. Metal in particular
suits these structures well: light, appropriate at low pitch, and long-lived. If a
contractor quotes shingles on a shallow manufactured-home roof without discussing pitch,
that's worth questioning.
