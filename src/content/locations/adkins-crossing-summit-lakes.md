## Two newer parts of Norman, one shared roofing problem

Summit Lakes sits on the east side off 24th and Alameda, a neighbourhood built around
several fishable residential lakes with walking trails and a private park, mixing established
homes with continuing new construction. Adkins Crossing is on the south side, close to both
I-35 and Highway 9 and a short run from downtown Norman.

Architecturally and geographically they have little in common. From a roofing perspective
they share the defining characteristic of newer Norman development: modern, uncomplicated
roof structures carrying coverings that were chosen by a builder to a budget, now old enough
that the first replacement is either due or approaching.

## The builder-grade problem, plainly

Production building is a price-driven business, and the roof is one of the easiest places to
save money without a buyer noticing. That usually means an entry-level shingle, standard
flashing details executed quickly, and ventilation installed to meet code rather than to
perform.

The shingle carries a long nominal warranty. That warranty describes performance under test
conditions, prorates steeply, and generally excludes hail, wind above a stated threshold and
anything attributable to inadequate ventilation. In central Oklahoma the practical result is
that these roofs commonly reach the end of their useful life between years 12 and 18.

This is not a scandal and it's not unique to any particular builder; it's how volume
housing works everywhere. It just means the roof on a 2008 house is a live question in 2026,
and many owners assume otherwise because the house still feels new.

## What we most often find on ten- to twenty-year-old Norman homes

**High nailing.** Shingles have a defined nailing zone. Nails placed above it hold one layer
instead of two. On a still day it makes no difference; in a 60 mph Oklahoma gust it makes
all of it. Completely invisible once the next course is laid.

**Thin or reused flashing.** Wall intersections, chimney chases and the sides of dormers.
Cheap to do badly, expensive to fix later, and the origin of most leaks on roofs that are
otherwise fine.

**Ventilation imbalance.** Ridge vent installed without sufficient soffit intake to feed it.
The ridge vent then pulls air from the path of least resistance, often conditioned air from
inside the house, instead of ventilating the attic. Common, easily fixed at replacement,
and quietly shortening roof life in the meantime.

**Uneven slope ageing.** With immature landscaping and full sun exposure, south and west
slopes can be visibly further gone than north-facing ones on the same house.

## Making the first replacement count

The first re-roof is the opportunity to correct what the builder didn't do. Full tear-off so
the decking gets inspected, all new flashing, balanced ventilation, and a shingle chosen for
this climate rather than for a spec sheet. Doing that once costs modestly more than a
like-for-like replacement and materially changes how long the next roof lasts.

Check your HOA covenants before you choose a material, then get the roof properly assessed. A

this year's budget or a problem for five years' time.
