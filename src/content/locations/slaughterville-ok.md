## A town that is mostly land

Slaughterville is an unusual municipality by any standard: roughly thirty-nine square miles
of south central Cleveland County holding a little over four thousand people. It incorporated
in 1970, took its name from James Slaughter's early-twentieth-century grocery store, and has
kept an emphatically rural land-use pattern ever since: acreages, ranches and small farms
rather than subdivisions.

For roofing that means something specific. There is no typical Slaughterville roof, because
there was never a builder putting up fifty of the same house. Every property was built
individually, at a different time, to a different standard, and almost all of them have
several structures.

## Two roofing systems on one property

**The house** is normally asphalt shingle and fails the way any Oklahoma roof fails: sun on
the south and west slopes, hail impacts, and wind breaking the seal strips that bond each
shingle to the one below. With no windbreak, the wind reaching a house out here has more
energy than the same wind reaching one in a sheltered neighbourhood, so seal failure and
creasing are proportionally more common.

**The outbuildings** are a different problem entirely. Exposed-fastener metal roofing is
essentially a set of panels held down by several thousand screws, each of which is a hole
through the panel sealed only by a rubber washer under the screw head. Those washers degrade
in ultraviolet light. The panels expand and contract through Oklahoma's temperature range,
which backs the screws out a fraction at a time. Eventually every fastener is a slow leak.

This is why the single most common, and most useful, piece of rural roofing work is a full
refasten: larger-diameter screws driven into fresh material, new closures at ridge and eave,
sealant renewed at panel laps and penetrations. On a structurally sound building it buys many
years for a small fraction of what re-sheeting costs, and a good provider would far rather
tell you that than sell a new roof that isn't needed.

## Damage here goes unnoticed for longer

In a Norman neighbourhood, a lifted shingle gets spotted by somebody. On a hundred-acre
parcel, a lifted panel on the back slope of a hay barn gets spotted when the hay gets wet.

That delay is the expensive part. A fastener problem caught early is a maintenance job.
The same problem two years later has admitted enough water to rust panels from underneath and
rot or corrode the purlins they're fixed to, and at that point it genuinely is a replacement.

The countermeasure is boring and effective: walk every roof on the property once a year, and
again after any significant hail or wind event. Look for lifted panel edges, missing or
proud screws, displaced ridge closures, and daylight visible from inside the building. If
you'd rather someone else did it, requesting a [roof inspection](/services/roof-inspection/)
can cover every structure on the parcel in a single visit and leave you with dated
photographs of each one.

## Working on a working property

A few things worth expecting on acreage jobs: gates left as they were found, debris contained
at the structure rather than dropped, containers and vehicles kept clear of pasture, drain
fields and septic laterals, and the working area magnet-swept at the end of each day. Loose
nails in a field are a real hazard to livestock and tyres, and treating that as an
afterthought is one of the clearer signals that a contractor doesn't normally work rural
property.
