## Wind is the local variable

Every part of Norman gets hail. What's different west of I-35 is the wind, and it's a
consequence of geography rather than weather patterns: open ground, young or absent tree
cover, and buildings that sit exposed rather than sheltered.

Wind damage is chronically under-diagnosed because it is nearly invisible. Homeowners look
for missing shingles, find none, and conclude the roof came through fine. Meanwhile the
actual damage is:

**Broken seal strips.** Every asphalt shingle bonds to the one below via a heat-activated
adhesive strip. Once that bond breaks, the shingle is loose even though it looks perfectly
seated. It lifts in the next gust and drives rain underneath. You cannot see it from the
ground and you often can't see it from the roof either; you have to lift and check.

**Creasing.** A shingle folded back by wind and dropped into place carries a fracture across
the fibreglass mat along the fold. It looks like a shingle. It functions like a shingle with
a crack in it.

**Edge and corner lifting.** Rakes, eaves and hips take the highest wind loads. Damage
concentrates there first, and it's the first place we look on an exposed property.

**Ridge cap displacement.** Ridge caps are the most exposed component on the roof and the
first to go.

## Acreage properties: the roof is bigger than the house

Further west, lots get larger and the property picture changes. A ten-acre parcel with a
house, a shop, a barn and a couple of equipment sheds can easily have more square footage of
metal roofing than shingle.

Metal buildings on exposed sites fail predictably: fasteners back out or get over-driven so
the neoprene washer fails, seams and closures separate, and hail dents panels. Most of this
is repairable. A systematic refastening and seam repair is far cheaper than re-sheeting and
often the correct answer.

What matters is that it gets assessed at all. Outbuildings tend to get ignored until
something inside gets wet, and by then a cheap fastener problem has become a rust problem.

## After a storm, walk the whole property

A practical checklist for exposed West Norman properties after any significant wind or hail
event:

- Check gutters, downspouts, vent caps and the AC condenser fins for denting, the clearest
  available evidence of hail severity, and the first thing an adjuster looks at
- Look along the ridge line for displaced or missing cap shingles
- Check rake and eave edges for lifting
- Walk the outbuildings and look for loose fasteners, lifted panels and damaged closures
- Photograph everything, dated, before anything is touched or tidied
