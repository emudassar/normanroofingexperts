## A town inside a city

Hall Park is one of the more unusual pieces of Norman's history. Built from 1960 on the
grounds of the old Norman Country Club, it incorporated as its own town and stayed that way
for over forty years, with its own police department from 1981, roughly 1,100 residents and
just over a square mile of ground. In 2003 residents voted to dissolve the town and be
annexed into Norman, and that annexation is what carried Norman past the 100,000 population
mark.

The development's original pitch, Oklahoma's first all-electric community, with Ronald
Reagan on hand as honorary mayor for the 1962 grand opening in his General Electric role,
is a good story. It's also genuinely useful to a roofer, because it tells you what to look
for.

## What the history means on the roof

**Retrofitted flue penetrations.** All-electric homes don't need a flue through the roof.
Natural gas arrived later in the 1960s, and where it was retrofitted, penetrations were cut
into roofs that were never designed for them. These are consistently among the leakiest
details we find in this neighbourhood, and it's where we look first on a leak call.

**Original ventilation design.** Attic ventilation practice in 1960 was not what it is now.
Under-ventilated attics run extremely hot in an Oklahoma July and degrade shingles from
underneath, which is a leading cause of roofs failing years short of their rated life, and
inadequate ventilation voids most manufacturers' material warranties. Correcting intake and
exhaust during a replacement is inexpensive; doing it afterwards means disturbing a new roof.

**Accumulated decking history.** Three or four generations of roof coverings on early plywood
sheathing leaves a deck with a lot of nail holes and, in places, reduced holding power.
Sometimes sections need replacing. It's a normal finding rather than a crisis, but it belongs
in the quote as a rate rather than as a surprise.

**Shared-space access.** The common-area backyard layout that makes Hall Park pleasant to
live in makes it awkward to stage a roofing job. It's entirely workable; it just has to be
agreed in advance.

## Straightforward roofs, older buildings

The roofs themselves are mostly simple. Modest single-family homes with conventional gable
and hip structures at moderate pitch are among the more economical roofs to replace, and
Hall Park jobs generally price at the lower end of the Norman range as a result.

The cost driver here isn't complexity, it's age. Decking remediation, ventilation upgrades
and properly redone retrofit penetrations are what move a Hall Park quote, and they are all
things worth doing rather than skipping, because a new roof on a sixty-year-old building fails at
the old building's weak points, not at the new shingles'.
