## Why central Oklahoma is different

Norman sits inside a stretch of the southern Plains that sees severe hail with a frequency
few parts of the country match. Peak season runs roughly March through June, though damaging
storms occur outside it. The practical result is that most roofs in Cleveland County will be
hit by a claimable event at some point in their service life, and many are hit more than once.

This changes the calculus in two ways. First, the roof you install should be specified for
this, which is why Class 4 impact-resistant shingles are worth a serious conversation here
even though they cost more. Second, storm restoration in this market is largely an insurance
process, and the contractors who serve homeowners well are the ones who understand claims,
not just shingles.

## What hail actually does to a roof

Hail damage is described as **functional** or **cosmetic**, and the distinction decides
claims.

**Functional damage** compromises the roof's ability to shed water. On asphalt shingles it
looks like random-pattern impact points where granules have been driven off and the
underlying mat is bruised or fractured. Press it and it gives slightly, like a bruise on
fruit. Once the mat is broken, water and UV reach the asphalt directly and that spot ages far
faster than the rest of the roof. Crucially, it may not leak for a year or more, and the damage
is real long before the symptom appears.

**Cosmetic damage** marks the surface without compromising performance. Some carriers now
attach cosmetic damage exclusions or separate wind/hail deductibles to policies in hail-prone
states. It's worth checking whether yours has one before a storm rather than after.

The tell that distinguishes hail from age is the pattern. Hail is random and correlates with
the storm's direction, and the north and west slopes typically take the worst of a north-westerly
storm. Wear is uniform and correlates with sun exposure. Blistering, which is frequently
misidentified as hail (sometimes honestly, sometimes not), is round, evenly distributed, and
is a ventilation or manufacturing issue rather than an insurable event.

## The claim process, honestly

The single most useful thing you can do is document early. Photograph dented gutters, AC fins
and downspouts yourself, on the day, before anyone touches anything. Note the date of the
storm. That five minutes is worth more than anything anyone does later.

Then get an independent assessment before you file. If the damage is marginal, a claim may
not be in your interest, and it is better to know that at the start.

When the adjuster comes, be there, and have your contractor there. Adjusters are working
through an enormous volume after a regional hail event and they are not infallible. Damage on
a steep or awkward slope gets missed. Two people on the roof at the same time, agreeing what
they're looking at, prevents most disputes before they start.

Expect supplements. Initial scopes routinely omit code-required items like ice-and-water
membrane, ventilation upgrades, decking replacement and full flashing. This is normal and it
is not adversarial. It is documented, filed, and usually approved.

## Watch for storm chasers

After every significant hail event in Norman, out-of-state crews arrive, knock doors, and
leave once the work is done. Some are competent. The risk is not workmanship on the day; it
is that your workmanship warranty is worthless the moment they leave the state, and roofing
defects typically surface a year or two later.

Warning signs worth taking seriously:

- Pressure to sign anything on your doorstep
- Any offer involving your deductible
- No verifiable local presence or Oklahoma phone number
- A price given without anyone having been on the roof
- A demand for substantial payment up front

Ask any contractor for proof of general liability and workers' compensation insurance
before they set foot on your property, and verify it independently. If someone is hurt on
your roof and the contractor isn't properly insured, that can become your problem.
