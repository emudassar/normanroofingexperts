## Low-slope roofs fail differently

A pitched residential roof sheds water quickly; the shingles' job is to get it moving
downhill. A low-slope commercial roof holds water, sometimes for hours, and the membrane's
job is to be genuinely watertight rather than water-shedding. That single difference drives
everything else.

**Seams are the weak point.** A single-ply membrane is only as good as the welds between
sheets and the terminations at edges, curbs and penetrations. Most commercial leaks are seam
or detail failures, not field failures.

**Drainage is a design issue.** Drains block, decks deflect over time, and insulation gets
compressed under foot traffic. Water finds the low spot and sits there. Ponding is a symptom
of drainage, and correcting drainage is often the highest-value work on the roof.

**Wet insulation is permanent damage.** Once insulation below the membrane is saturated, it
does not dry out under a sealed roof. It loses R-value permanently, holds moisture against
the deck, and keeps attacking the membrane from underneath. This is why a roof that has been
leaking quietly for two years costs far more than one caught at six months.

**Foot traffic accumulates.** HVAC service, telecom work and window cleaning all put people
on the roof, usually with tools and no walkway pads. It's a common and entirely preventable
cause of membrane damage.

## Restoration versus replacement

Property owners are often told they need a full replacement when the roof is a restoration
candidate. The honest test is what's underneath.

If the deck is sound and the insulation is dry, a **restoration coating**, silicone or
acrylic over a properly prepared substrate, can add a meaningful number of years for a
fraction of replacement cost, usually with a manufacturer warranty attached, and without
tear-off, disposal or business disruption.

If the insulation is wet across a significant area, or the deck is compromised, restoration
is money spent on a roof that will fail underneath the new coating. A moisture survey
distinguishes the two cases, and it's worth doing before committing six figures either way.

## Building types we work on in Norman

Norman's light commercial stock has a few recognisable patterns. Older buildings around the
downtown core frequently have built-up roofs over board decks, sometimes with several layers
of history above them and parapet details that need careful attention. Suburban retail and
office along the main corridors is largely single-ply over metal deck. Churches often combine
a steep, highly visible shingled or metal main roof with low-slope sections over halls and
extensions: two systems on one building, meeting at a detail that is usually the first thing
to leak. Multi-family and student-oriented housing near the university tends toward
residential-style pitched roofs at commercial scale, where the constraint is scheduling
around tenants rather than the roofing itself.

## Documentation for owners and boards

Where a decision has to go to a board, an HOA, a church committee or an absent owner, the
survey report is written to be usable by people who will never go on the roof: photographs
with locations marked, condition summary, remaining-life estimate, and a prioritised cost
schedule separating what needs doing this year from what can be planned for later. Getting a
capital expenditure approved is usually a documentation problem, and we'd rather solve it
properly than send a one-page quote into a committee meeting.
