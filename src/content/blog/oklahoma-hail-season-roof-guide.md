If you own a house in Norman, your roof will meet hail. Not as a remote possibility, but as a
scheduled event with an uncertain date. Central Oklahoma sits inside one of the most active
severe-hail corridors in the United States, and most roofs in Cleveland County will be struck
by a claimable event at some point during their service life. Plenty are struck more than once.

This guide covers what actually happens to a roof when hail hits it, how to distinguish real
damage from ordinary wear, and what to do in the first two days after a storm, which is when
most of the decisions that matter get made or missed.

## When hail season runs

Severe hail in central Oklahoma is concentrated roughly from March through June, with the
peak in spring. That's the pattern, not a guarantee: damaging hail happens outside those
months and some springs pass quietly.

Two practical implications. First, if you're planning a roof replacement, doing it before
spring means the new roof faces the season rather than the old one. Second, autumn is
generally the easiest time to get a roofer's full attention in this market, because everyone
is booked solid from April onward.

## What hail actually does to asphalt shingles

An asphalt shingle is a fibreglass mat saturated with asphalt and surfaced with mineral
granules. Each layer has a job. The granules block ultraviolet light. The asphalt provides
waterproofing. The mat provides strength.

When a hailstone hits hard enough, it knocks granules off and fractures the mat underneath.
That combination is what roofers call **functional damage**: the UV shield is gone and the
structure is broken, so that spot now ages several times faster than the rest of the roof.

The critical thing to understand is the delay. A hail-bruised shingle usually does not leak
immediately. It leaks in a year, or two, or three, once UV has degraded the exposed asphalt
at the impact point. Homeowners routinely conclude they escaped a storm because nothing came
through the ceiling. What actually happened is that the clock started.

**Cosmetic damage**, by contrast, marks the surface without compromising performance. The
distinction matters because a growing number of policies in hail-prone states carry cosmetic
damage exclusions or separate wind and hail deductibles. Worth reading your policy before a
storm rather than after.

## Telling hail damage from ordinary wear

The single most useful diagnostic is **pattern**.

**Hail is random and directional.** Impacts are scattered with no regularity, and they
correlate with the direction the storm came from, so a north-westerly storm damages north and
west slopes hardest. Impact points are soft to the touch, like a bruise on fruit.

**Wear is uniform and correlates with sun.** South and west slopes weather fastest because
they get the most sun. Granule loss from age is even across a slope rather than spotted.

**Blistering is round, evenly distributed, and not hail.** It's a manufacturing or
ventilation issue and it is not an insurable event. It gets misidentified as hail
constantly, sometimes honestly and sometimes not, by contractors who benefit from the
confusion.

The corroborating evidence is on the **soft metals**, and it's the first thing an adjuster
looks at. If a storm was severe enough to damage shingles, it will have dented gutters,
downspouts, vent caps, window wraps and the fins of your air conditioning condenser. Those
dents are much easier to see than roof damage, and they are the reason a claim gets taken
seriously. If the soft metals around your house are untouched, be sceptical of anyone telling
you the roof is destroyed.

## The first 48 hours: a checklist

**1. Stay off the roof.** A wet or hail-strewn roof is genuinely dangerous, and you can gather
most of what you need from the ground.

**2. Photograph the soft metals, dated, before anything is touched.** Gutters, downspouts,
vent caps, window wraps, the AC condenser, mailbox, fence caps, garden furniture. This is your
evidence that the storm was severe. Do it before you tidy up.

**3. Note the date and time of the storm.** Insurance claims are about a specific event on a
specific date. "Sometime last spring" is a much weaker position than a date.

**4. Check inside.** Look at ceilings, particularly in upstairs rooms and around chimneys and
vents. Get into the attic with a torch and look for daylight, damp decking or wet insulation.

**5. Mitigate active water immediately.** If water is coming in, stopping it is both urgent
and usually claimable as a separate mitigation cost. Buckets and tarps now save interior
repairs later, and most policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further
damage.

**6. Get an independent inspection before you file.** Not after. If the damage is marginal, a
claim may not be in your interest, and it's better to know that before it's on your record.

**7. Do not sign anything on your doorstep.** Storm chasing spikes in central Oklahoma after
every significant event. A legitimate contractor will still be available tomorrow.

## What to be wary of afterwards

After a regional hail event, out-of-state crews arrive in volume. Some are competent. The
problem isn't necessarily the workmanship on the day; it's that a workmanship warranty from
a company that has left the state is worth nothing, and roofing defects typically surface a
year or two later.

Specific things that should end a conversation:

- **Any offer involving your deductible.** Rebating, absorbing or advertising the waiving of
  an insurance deductible is fraud under Oklahoma law, and it exposes you as well as the
  contractor.
- **Pressure to sign immediately.** Particularly a contingency agreement, which commits you
  to a contractor before you know the scope or the price.
- **A price quoted without anyone having been on the roof.**
- **No verifiable Oklahoma presence.** Ask for the Construction Industries Board registration
  number and check it. Ask for certificates of general liability and workers' compensation
  insurance, and verify them independently. If someone is hurt on your roof and the
  contractor isn't properly insured, that can become your problem.

## Reducing the damage next time

You cannot hail-proof a roof, and anyone who says otherwise is selling something. You can
reduce the odds and the cost.

**Class 4 impact-resistant shingles** are tested against steel-ball impact and hold up
measurably better against small and moderate hail. They will still be damaged by large stones.
Their strongest argument in this market is often financial rather than physical: many Oklahoma
insurers offer a premium discount for them that recovers a meaningful share of the extra cost
over the roof's life. Ask your carrier what discount applies to you specifically, because it varies,
and it changes the calculation.

**Keep the roof in good condition generally.** A roof already at year 18 with brittle shingles
sustains worse damage than a healthy one, and a carrier is far more likely to argue that what
you're claiming is age rather than hail.

**Know your policy before the storm.** Replacement cost value versus actual cash value makes an
enormous difference to what you receive. So does a percentage-based wind and hail deductible.
Neither is a pleasant thing to discover during a claim.
