Oklahoma asks more of a roof than most places. Hail in spring, straight-line winds that
regularly reach speeds other states treat as remarkable, summer attic temperatures that cook
asphalt from underneath, and periodic ice events that load structures and bring branches down.

Any material can survive one of those. Choosing well means asking how it handles all four,
and being honest about the fact that the warranty on the box was not written with Oklahoma in
mind.

## First, about warranty numbers

A "30-year" shingle is not a shingle that lasts thirty years in Norman. It's a shingle that
performed to a standard under controlled laboratory conditions, sold with a warranty that
prorates steeply after the first several years, generally excludes hail, excludes wind above a
stated speed, and is voided by inadequate attic ventilation.

Realistic service life in central Oklahoma is meaningfully shorter than the number on the
packaging, for every asphalt product. That isn't a criticism of the manufacturers; it's what
happens when you take a product rated in a laboratory and install it in a hail corridor.

Plan around realistic figures rather than warranty figures, and the rest of the decision gets
much clearer.

## Architectural asphalt shingles

**Realistic life here: roughly 15–22 years. Cost: moderate.**

This is the default across Cleveland County and it deserves to be. Architectural (or
dimensional) shingles are laminated from multiple layers, which makes them heavier, better
wind-rated and considerably more durable than the old three-tab product they replaced.

**Hail:** moderate resistance. They'll be damaged by significant hail, and after a bad storm
you're likely making a claim.

**Wind:** good when installed correctly. The qualifier matters more than the rating, because most
wind failures are nailing failures, not shingle failures. A shingle nailed above its nailing
zone holds one layer instead of two.

**Heat:** acceptable, provided the attic is properly ventilated. This is the most
under-appreciated variable in Oklahoma roofing.

**Ice:** fine. Weight isn't an issue and asphalt handles freeze-thaw well.

**The strongest argument for asphalt is repairability.** Every roofer in the state carries it,
a repair is cheap, and after a claim the replacement is straightforward. In a market where
your roof will probably be damaged at some point, "easy and inexpensive to fix or replace" is a
genuine engineering virtue rather than a consolation prize.

## Class 4 impact-resistant shingles

**Realistic life here: roughly 18–25 years. Cost: moderate-high, typically 10–20% above
standard architectural.**

Class 4 is the top rating under UL 2218, which tests shingles by dropping a two-inch steel
ball from twenty feet and checking for mat fracture. Products achieve it through modified
asphalt formulations, a polymer backing, or heavier construction.

**Hail:** the best asphalt option available. Measurably better against small and moderate
hail. Not hail-proof, because large stones will still cause damage, and any roofer who claims
otherwise is selling.

**Wind, heat, ice:** as architectural asphalt or slightly better, since they're generally
heavier.

**The financial argument is often stronger than the physical one.** Many Oklahoma insurers
offer a homeowners premium discount for a Class 4 roof. Over fifteen to twenty years, that
discount can recover a large share, sometimes all, of the extra up-front cost. The size of
the discount varies by carrier and it is specific to your policy, so the correct move is to
call your agent and ask before deciding. That number, not the marketing, should drive the
choice.

**In this market, this is the option most Norman homeowners should at least price.**

## Metal roofing

**Realistic life here: 40–60 years for standing seam, 40+ for stone-coated steel. Cost:
highest.**

**Standing seam** conceals its fasteners under raised, mechanically seamed ribs. **Stone-coated
steel** uses steel panels with a granulated surface that reads more like tile or shake from the
ground. **Exposed-fastener corrugated panel** is the agricultural product: excellent value on
a barn, and the fastener-and-washer maintenance cycle discussed below is exactly why it's less
suited to a house.

**Hail:** structurally excellent, cosmetically variable. Metal very rarely fails functionally
under hail. It does dent visibly, and this is the crux of the decision. Some homeowners find
a dented roof unacceptable; others genuinely don't care. Critically, **many policies exclude
cosmetic damage on metal roofs**, so a dented but functional metal roof may not be a claim.
Check your policy language before you buy the roof, not after the storm.

**Wind:** outstanding. Standing seam is among the best wind-resistant residential systems
available, which counts for a lot on exposed sites west and south of Norman.

**Heat:** very good. Reflective finishes reduce cooling load noticeably over an Oklahoma
summer, particularly on large roof areas.

**Ice:** excellent, and it sheds snow and ice readily, worth thinking about above doorways
and walkways.

**Consider metal if** you plan to stay in the house long-term, you're on an exposed site, or
you have outbuildings where it's already the obvious answer. **Be cautious if** you're likely
to move within a decade, since you won't recover the premium, or if visible denting would bother
you.

## Three-tab asphalt shingles

**Realistic life here: 12–18 years. Cost: lowest.**

Largely superseded. Lower wind rating, thinner, shorter-lived, and the saving over
architectural shingles is modest. It still appears on rental property and in the cheapest
bids. In a hail and wind market there's very little argument for choosing it deliberately.

## Low-slope systems: TPO and modified bitumen

**Realistic life here: 15–25 years depending on system. Cost: varies widely.**

Not a whole-house choice for most homes, but relevant to a great many Norman properties
because porches, additions, dormers and patio covers frequently have sections below the
minimum slope for shingles.

This matters more than it sounds. **Shingles laid on a low-slope section will leak**, and not
because of poor workmanship but because they rely on gravity to shed water and there isn't
enough of it. Wind-driven rain gets underneath. It's one of the most common avoidable defects
we find, particularly on older homes with enclosed porches and rear extensions.

If part of your roof is nearly flat, it needs a membrane system. A contractor who proposes
shingling it should be asked why.

**TPO** is a single-ply membrane, usually white, with heat-welded seams, reflective, which
helps considerably in summer. **Modified bitumen** is a multi-ply asphalt system, robust and
well suited to areas with foot traffic.

## The part nobody chooses, which matters more than the material

**Ventilation.** An Oklahoma attic without balanced intake and exhaust runs far hotter than
outside air in July, degrading asphalt from underneath. This is a leading cause of roofs
failing years short of rating, and inadequate ventilation voids most manufacturers' material
warranties, meaning the warranty you were sold on may already be void.

Balanced means both halves. Ridge vent without adequate soffit intake doesn't work; it just
pulls air from the easiest available path, sometimes conditioned air from inside your house.

**Underlayment and flashing.** Synthetic underlayment across the field, ice-and-water membrane
in valleys and around penetrations, and all-new flashing. Almost every leak on an otherwise
sound roof is a flashing detail, and reusing flashing is the easiest thousand dollars to shave
off a bid invisibly.

**Nailing.** Four to six nails per shingle, in the nailing zone, driven flush, not
overdriven, not proud, not high. Invisible the moment the next course goes down, and the
single most common cause of premature wind failure.

A cheap shingle installed properly on a well-ventilated roof with new flashing will outlast a
premium shingle installed badly. Every time.

## Quick comparison

| Material | Realistic life here | Cost | Hail | Wind | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Three-tab asphalt | 12–18 yrs | Lowest | Poor | Fair | Rentals, tight budgets |
| Architectural asphalt | 15–22 yrs | Moderate | Moderate | Good | Most Norman homes |
| Class 4 impact-resistant | 18–25 yrs | Moderate-high | Best asphalt option | Good | Hail country, insurance discount |
| Stone-coated steel | 40+ yrs | High | Excellent function, may dent | Excellent | Long-term ownership |
| Standing seam metal | 40–60 yrs | Highest | Excellent function, may dent | Excellent | Exposed sites, long-term |
| TPO / modified bitumen | 15–25 yrs | Varies | Good | Good | Low-slope sections |
