A roof insurance claim is not really a construction process. It's a documentation process
with construction at the end of it, and most of what determines the outcome happens before
anyone picks up a nail gun.

This is the whole sequence, in order, with the parts that catch people out flagged as they
come up. It's written for Oklahoma homeowners; we're roofers rather than licensed insurance
advisers, so treat this as an explanation of how the process runs in practice, and take
policy-specific questions to your agent.

## Before anything: know what your policy says

Two terms decide how much money you actually receive, and most people learn them during a
claim rather than before one.

**RCV vs ACV.** A **replacement cost value** policy pays what it costs to replace the roof.
An **actual cash value** policy pays what your old roof was worth, meaning replacement cost minus
depreciation for its age and condition. On a fifteen-year-old roof, that difference can be
many thousands of dollars, and it is the single largest variable in most claims. ACV policies
are cheaper for a reason.

**Your deductible, and whether it's a percentage.** Many policies in hail-prone states carry a
separate wind and hail deductible expressed as a percentage of the dwelling's insured value
rather than a flat sum. On a $350,000 home, a 2% wind and hail deductible is $7,000, which
can exceed the entire cost of the repair you were about to claim for.

Find your declarations page and check both. Fifteen minutes now saves an unpleasant surprise
later.

## Step 1: Document before you file

Photograph everything, dated, before anything is touched or tidied. Dented gutters,
downspouts, vent caps, window wraps and AC condenser fins are the evidence that the storm was
severe, and they're what an adjuster looks at first, and they're visible from the ground.

Note the date of the storm. A claim is about a specific event on a specific date.

## Step 2: Get an independent inspection first

Before you file, not after. Two reasons.

If the damage is marginal, or is age rather than storm, a claim may not be in your
interest. Even a denied claim can appear on your record, and some carriers count claim
frequency at renewal regardless of outcome. Knowing what you actually have before you pick up
the phone is worth the hour it takes.

And if the damage is real, you enter the process with your own documentation rather than
relying entirely on someone else's assessment.

A good inspection produces dated, captioned, slope-by-slope photographs with measurements,
the format adjusters expect to receive.

## Step 3: File the claim

You file it. Your policy is a contract between you and your carrier, and no contractor should
be filing on your behalf. What a contractor provides is documentation and the storm date, so
you can file accurately.

You'll be given a claim number and, usually within a few days, an adjuster and an appointment.
After a large regional event that timeline stretches, sometimes considerably, because
carriers are handling thousands of claims from the same storm.

## Step 4: The adjuster inspection

**Be there. Have your contractor there.**

This is the most consequential hour of the process and it's the one homeowners most often
skip. Adjusters after a regional hail event are working through enormous volume under time
pressure. They are not infallible, and damage on a steep, awkward or shaded slope gets missed
in good faith.

Two qualified people on the roof at the same time, looking at the same test squares and
agreeing what they're seeing, prevents most disputes before they exist. A disagreement
resolved on the roof takes ten minutes. The same disagreement resolved afterwards takes weeks
of correspondence.

## Step 5: The scope and the first payment

The adjuster produces a **scope**, an itemised estimate of the work and its cost. If the
claim is approved, the first payment follows, and what arrives depends on your policy type.

**On an RCV policy**, the first cheque is usually the actual cash value: the full replacement
cost minus your deductible and minus **recoverable depreciation**. That depreciation is held
back and released after the work is completed and documented. This catches people out
constantly: the first payment looks far too small, and homeowners assume the claim was
underpaid when in fact the balance is being held pending completion.

**On an ACV policy**, the depreciation is not recoverable. What you receive is what you get,
and you fund the gap.

## Step 6: Supplements

Initial scopes are frequently incomplete. This is normal rather than adversarial, because adjusters
estimate from what they can see and from standard line items, and roofs reveal things once
they're opened.

Common omissions: code-required ice-and-water membrane, ventilation upgrades required by
current code, decking replacement, full flashing replacement, drip edge, and the correct
number of layers to be removed.

A **supplement** is a documented request to add these to the approved scope. Filed with
photographs and code references, supplements are routinely approved. The alternative, a
contractor quietly cutting scope to fit an inadequate estimate, is how you end up with new
shingles over old flashing.

Be direct with any contractor about this: ask whether they file supplements, or whether they
absorb shortfalls by reducing what gets done.

## Step 7: The work, and releasing depreciation

The roof gets built. You pay your deductible, and only your deductible, to your contractor,
in the normal way.

On an RCV policy, your contractor's final invoice and completion documentation go to the
carrier, and the held-back recoverable depreciation is released. Keep all of it: invoices,
completion photographs, warranty registrations, permits. Carriers ask for it and claims stall
when it's missing.

## What to do if the claim is denied

A denial is not necessarily final. Claims get denied because damage was missed on a difficult
slope, because documentation was thin, or because the adjuster judged the damage cosmetic
rather than functional.

Your options, roughly in order:

1. **Request a re-inspection**, with additional documentation supporting what you believe was
   missed.
2. **Submit a second qualified opinion** with its own photographic evidence.
3. **Invoke the appraisal clause.** Most policies contain one. Each side appoints an
   appraiser; if they disagree, an umpire decides. It's a contractual dispute mechanism that
   avoids litigation.
4. **Contact the Oklahoma Insurance Department** if you believe the claim has been handled
   improperly.

And sometimes the denial is simply right, and the roof is worn out rather than storm damaged. A
contractor who tells you that, rather than encouraging you to appeal a correct decision, is
one worth keeping.

## Three things that will hurt you

**Any offer involving your deductible.** Rebating, absorbing or advertising the waiving of an
insurance deductible is fraud under Oklahoma law and it exposes you as well as the contractor.
A contractor willing to commit fraud on your behalf at the start of a job is not one whose
warranty means anything at the end of it.

**Signing a contingency agreement under pressure.** These commit you to a contractor if the
claim is approved, before you know the scope or the price. Some are perfectly reasonable. Some
carry steep cancellation penalties. Read it, and don't sign it on your doorstep.

**Waiting.** Policies set notice deadlines and they vary: some require notice within a year
of the date of loss. Beyond the formal deadline, damage that goes unreported for a long time
becomes hard to attribute to a specific storm, and carriers reasonably ask why nobody noticed
for two years.
